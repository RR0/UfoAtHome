# Testimony evaluation leads

Ideas for assessing a sighting's testimony and explaining it, drawn from published studies of how
witnesses perceive, remember and report. None is implemented yet. Each lead lists the studies behind
it, so its data and caveats can be checked there before it becomes an `Assessor` or a candidate check.

No single source is taken as authoritative. Each lead carries a status:

- **consolidated**: measured by at least two independent studies (different teams, different data);
- **single study**: measured once, not replicated;
- **argued**: reasoned or illustrated by cases, not measured;
- **contested**: sources disagree; both sides are given.

Sources are cited by key (e.g. `[Hartmann 1968]`); the [bibliography](#bibliography) gives the full
reference and where to read it on rr0.org. Several chapters of the 2023 collection
`[RUWT 2023]` summarise earlier studies: the earlier study is cited, and the chapter only as
the place it was summarised. Where two sources look independent but are not (same author, same team,
same data), it is said.

## Distances, sizes and speeds

### Metres are not data; angles, counts and layout are
**Status: consolidated.**
- Zond IV re-entry (1968-03-03), about 30 usable reports: angular size was given consistently
  (3-4 arcmin per fragment); size, distance and speed were wrong by more than a factor of 10;
  estimates ranged from treetop level to above 60,000 ft for an object ~100 mi up `[Hartmann 1968]`.
  The 1913-02-09 fireball procession: distance "grossly underestimated" `[Hartmann 1968]` after
  `[Chant 1913]`.
- ~300 police reports of 7 atmospheric entries (1980-2009): stated altitude and distance follow
  lognormal laws with medians of 300 m and 650 m (σ(ln) ≈ 1.7) whatever the truth (80-120 km
  altitude); reliability about 0 for altitude, distance and heading, 0.5 to ~1 for date, time,
  duration, fragmentation and colours `[Rospars 2023]`.
- Phoenix 1997 and Zond IV: 68 % agree on the number of lights, 87 % on the layout, 79 % on the
  heading; size, altitude and speed systematically too low, too big, too slow `[Bullard 2014]`
  (repeated in `[Bullard 2023]`: same author, not an independent confirmation).
- 1,225 gendarmerie testimonies of 18 re-entries 1974-1990: only 11 % give a distance in metres or
  km, 24 % an altitude, 21 % a size, all for objects 50-100 km up; their error is inferred from the
  stimulus height, not measured per testimony `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 220-238).
- Hendry never asked a distance; all 33 of his "close encounters" that turned out to be identified
  (20 advertising planes, 6 stars...) exist only because witnesses volunteered distances under
  500 ft `[Hendry 1979]`. How many give a distance at all depends on the file: 5 % of one French
  catalogue `[Besse 1980]`, 39 % of identified and 68 % of unidentified gendarmerie reports
  `[Besse 1981]`, `[Besse 1982]` via `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 45-57).
- Nothing tells the eye how far a light is beyond about 20 m (convergence, accommodation), and
  binocular disparity only to a few hundred metres for a sharp point `[Jimenez 1994]` (ch. 4,
  reviewing Bourdon 1902, Graham 1962, Cutting): the distance a witness gives comes from the scene or
  from what they took the light to be (Hastorf 1950), not from the light.
- Mechanism: the retinal image fixes the angle, not size, distance or speed; the observer infers one
  from an assumed size `[Wertheimer 1968]`. Any distance to an unknown light beyond about 20 m is
  random (expert statement, not a study) `[Passot 2023]`.
- 1990-11-05 re-entry: crews saw a "collision course" with debris tens of miles up; a slow angular
  rate was read as slow speed because the distance was underestimated `[Oberg 1994]`. Yukon 1996:
  a witness triangulation gave a craft 0.5-1 mile across; the stimulus was a re-entry `[Bullard 2014]`.

**Use**: never derive metres from a stated distance. Constrain the scene with angles and timing;
use metres only when anchored (a known-size object, occlusion by terrain or cloud at a known
distance, triangulation between independent, distant witnesses, see
[parallax](#far-or-near-parallax-between-distant-witnesses)).

### Stated angular size is too large, but it is often the spread of lights
**Status: consolidated for the overestimate, contested for its size.**
- 238 people sized the Moon at arm's length at 7.05 cm on average against 0.5 cm; typical answer
  1.6-2 cm (3-4×); bright objects judged larger `[Peiniger 1998a]`.
- A plane drawn 30 times bigger than it was (GEIPAN case, Silly-le-Long 2015); people cover the
  rising Moon with "5 fingers" or "2 hands" (15°, ×30) `[Passot 2023]` (one case and an informal test).
- Against: under good conditions, sizes shown by hand right away were within +2 to +17 %; only a size
  recalled through a familiar object (a football) was ~30 % too large `[Keul 2015]`. Zond IV
  witnesses gave consistent angular sizes; a third gave more than 7 arcmin `[Hartmann 1968]`.
  Keul also analysed `[Peiniger 1998b]`: the German studies are not fully independent.
- For aircraft, Hendry's size classes "3" and "4" were mostly the spread between the outermost lights
  of a formation, not a body; a star watched for 90 min "grew" to full-Moon size `[Hendry 1979]`.

**Use**: ask each witness to show the full Moon the way they showed the object; the ratio is their own
scale error. Otherwise treat an unanchored size as an upper bound with a wide, heavy-tailed error.
Record whether the size was shown on the spot or recalled through an object. For a night shape, ask
whether a body was seen or only lights.

### Speed words are not speeds
**Status: single study** `[Hendry 1979]`.
"Dropped like a shot" meant 30 s to 2 min; "took off like a cannonball", 60 s; "zigzagged" around a
star was autokinesis over 40 min to 2 h. A car chase ("it outran us at 110 mph") says nothing
against an aircraft.

**Use**: store the witness's word apart from the timed duration; ask how long it took, not how fast.

## Direction and elevation

### Elevation is overestimated, asymmetrically
**Status: consolidated.**
- Near 0° and 90° elevations are overestimated; anything above 30-45° is often reported as
  "overhead" `[Wertheimer 1968]`.
- 196 identified astronomical cases: for estimates of 15-25°, 9 % of the true objects were below 4°
  and 38 % at 4-8°; for 25-40°, 42 % were at 10° or lower `[van Utrecht 2023]` (unpublished study,
  not replicated).

**Use**: widen the tolerance downward, not symmetrically; read "overhead" as 30° or more; "at treetop
level" beats a number given afterwards.

### Low lights are placed at the horizon, less and less as they rise
**Status: consolidated.**
- Experiments `[Jimenez 1994]` (ch. 5): a 31′ light projected 1°, 6°, 13° or 35° above skylines 12,
  100 and 400 m away (two groups of about 25 subjects) was placed near the skyline's distance when low
  (1°: about 25, 80 and 570-670 m), and the skyline's pull decreased with elevation but stayed
  significant at 35°; estimates converged toward 100-300 m as the light rose, a near skyline pushing
  them beyond it and a far one pulling them in front. Fitted on his tables (not a formula he gives):
  log D ≈ a + b·log H with b ≈ 0.9 at 1°, 0.65 at 6°, 0.4 at 13°, 0.25 at 35°. The real full Moon,
  judged by 64 passers-by asked to see it as a mere light: median 1⅓ times the skyline distance when
  low; the point where it would "fall" was placed beyond it.
- There is no 5° threshold: that was a split of the Moon elevations sampled, made after the fact
  (p. 111), and it is how `[Maugé 2023]` summarised the result.
- Field data `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 195-201, 247 testimonies of stars, planets and two re-entries):
  below 10° of true elevation, 51-62 % of witnesses gave the distance against the landscape, and 76 %
  of those put it at or nearer than the horizon; at 10-30°, 16-20 % did.
- Mechanism: a vague light takes the distance of what surrounds it (equidistance tendency,
  `[Gogel 1965]`), and the sky is seen as a flattened vault, halved at about 30° of elevation
  `[Jimenez 1994]` (ch. 4); the Moon illusion grows with the distance of the skyline
  (`[Rock 1962]`).
- A meteor "landed just behind the barn"; a Titan 3C-4 re-entry placed in front of mountains a few
  miles away; the 1913 procession "just above the hills" `[Hartmann 1968]`. The 1965 Great Lakes
  fireball "just behind the trees" `[Wetherill 1966]` via `[Young 2023]`.
- A setting Moon "landed 1 or 2 miles away"; setting Venus "landed at the school" `[Hendry 1979]`.

**Use**: compute the skyline distance H along the bearing from the DEM, and the light's elevation above
that skyline (not above the astronomical horizon); predict the distance the witness would give and
treat a reported distance inside that band (about ±50 %) as uninformative, still testing far
candidates. A claimed landing point is not a position. Ask "was it farther or nearer than the
buildings or trees behind it, and how many times?", and the witness's own estimate of the skyline
distance, to calibrate them. Compare a reported disappearance time with rise and set times against
the terrain horizon.

### Other cues to a light's distance
**Status: consolidated (laboratory); measured once for size.**
With no visible landscape (sea, fog, a black background), a lone light is seen very near, 1-5 m
(specific distance tendency, `[Gogel 1969]`); of two lights, the brighter looks nearer, and the
smaller farther (65 % of 266 answers in a Jimenez experiment at the 1981 Le Bourget air show); a light
growing slightly is seen approaching; the same object is judged twice as far in fog as in clear air
(Ross 1967); all via `[Jimenez 1994]` (ch. 4).

**Use**: flag scenes with no visible skyline, where "a few metres away" is a perceptual default;
predict "it came towards us" when a candidate brightens or grows in the rendering; scale expected
distances with the weather's visibility.

### Heading across the line of sight is reliable; the sense depends on where the witness stood
**Status: consolidated.**
- A distant horizontal track is seen moving across the line of sight, so "moved N→S" means the
  witness looked E or W, within ±45°; between witnesses only opposite headings contradict (2 %)
  `[Rospars 2023]`; heading error ±45°, some reversed `[Passot 2023]`.
- Wartime sailors: distances and bearings unreliable, port vs starboard reliable `[Jones 1968]` via
  `[Hartmann 1968]`.
- AMS fireball #2908: witnesses south of the track saw it pass left to right, those north right to
  left, those under it saw it fall vertically (rr0.org testimony page).
- A meteor can appear to go any direction, including horizontal or upward: "it went horizontal, so
  not a meteor" is a common false elimination `[Hendry 1979]`.

**Use**: for each witness position, compute the sense (left/right, falling) a candidate track would
show and score the reports against it.

### Far or near: parallax between distant witnesses
**Status: argued** `[Bullard 2014]`.
Yukon 1996: the same direction (north) and motion (W→E) along 200 miles of highway means the object was
far away; a near object would have to restage its appearance for each witness.

**Use**: for witnesses more than ~10 km apart, compare the bearings a near hypothesis implies with
those of a far one.

### Apparent motion that is not motion
**Status: consolidated for autokinesis (laboratory); argued or case-based for the rest.**
- A lone light on a structureless background soon seems to move (autokinesis, `[Guilford 1929]`,
  group convergence `[Sherif 1935]`) `[Wertheimer 1968]`, `[Rhine 1968]`; moving objects seen as
  stopped ("autostasis") `[Hartmann 1968]`; the Moon "races" behind moving cloud `[Bullard 2014]`.
- An aircraft coming toward or going away from the witness looks stationary: "4 lights for 5 min",
  "a DC-9 for 1 min" `[Hendry 1979]`.
- "Sudden departure" is often lights switching off (advertising sign, aircraft turning) `[Hendry 1979]`;
  a light going off seems to shrink; one light off as another comes on reads as motion
  `[Wertheimer 1968]`.
- A growing image can be something receding while it expands; a rising image can be a level path
  toward the observer; a disc looks like a line, cigar, ellipse or circle depending on orientation
  `[Wertheimer 1968]` (geometry).
- Brightening is read as approaching: a rocket receding the whole time "approached from over the
  horizon" (Tajik Air, 1994) `[Oberg 1994]` (one case).
- "Follower" lights: a star or Venus "followed our car" `[Hendry 1979]`, `[Hartmann 1968]`,
  `[Borraz 2023]`; a plane flying against a moving van looked stationary `[Passot 2023]`.
- Afterimages: after a bright flash in the dark, an afterimage can float with the gaze for a minute
  or more, changing colour `[Wertheimer 1968]`.

**Use**: flag "moved erratically" for a point light with no reference, "hovered then left",
"sequential lights moving"; test candidates whose bearing stays nearly constant for the stated hover
time; when a candidate's magnitude rises while its range grows, treat "it approached" as consistent
with it. With a moving observer, compute the candidate's bearing relative to the vehicle's heading
(test Venus, Jupiter, the Moon and bright stars within ~10° of the bearing).

## Time, date and duration

### Error laws
**Status: consolidated for duration; single study for the time law.**
- Date right for 98 %, otherwise the next day; time within about N(0, 6 min) for 90 % of witnesses,
  with a wider 10 % tail; duration lognormal around the true value, σ(ln) ≈ 1.25 (10 % report at
  least 5×) `[Rospars 2023]`.
- A 10 s photograph: estimates from 1.5 to 30 s, group means 7.5-12 s `[Peiniger 1998b]`.
- Yukon 1996: estimated times ran from 7 pm to 10 pm; the two witnesses who looked at a clock said
  8:23 and 8:30, matching the re-entry at ~8:30; durations from half a minute to ten minutes
  `[Bullard 2014]`.
- Duration is the variable least distorted by how a catalogue was selected: independent French
  catalogues pass a χ² homogeneity test on it `[Besse 1980]`.
- Events under ~30 min tend to be overestimated `[Tobin 2010]` via `[Ickinger 2023]` (not a UFO study).
- Contested for re-entries: "generally overestimated" `[Jimenez 1994]` (p. 221, no figure) against
  a lognormal centred on the true value `[Rospars 2023]` (measured).

**Use**: store a time source (clock, anchored to an event, estimated); match candidates tightly on
clock times and loosely on estimates (±30 min). Model one witness's duration as log-normal ×/÷3 and
tighten it only by averaging independent witnesses.

### Reference durations
**Status: consolidated.**
- Meteors: 79 % under 10 s, 52 % under 5 s `[Hendry 1979]`; median 8 s (3-20 s) `[Rospars 2023]`;
  the longest of 6 bright fireballs 31 s `[Hartmann 1968]` after `[Olivier 1962]`.
- Re-entries: up to 2-3 min, horizon to horizon in 10 s to 3 min `[Hendry 1979]`; Zond IV ~2 min
  `[Hartmann 1968]`; near-horizontal ones visible "many minutes", especially from aircraft
  `[Oberg 1994]`; median 35-42 s rests on a single launcher `[Rospars 2023]`.
- Launch plumes: 40-60 min, sunlit before dawn, twisted by winds `[Oberg 1994]`; Titan train ~6 min,
  dust trains photographed up to 17 min `[Hartmann 1968]`.
- Aircraft up to ~45 min, usually 10 min or less; prank balloons 1-15 min; stars hours
  `[Hendry 1979]`.

### A wrong date is a common error
**Status: argued, case-based.**
When a low red or orange "ball" does not match the Moon or a planet on the stated date, recompute
for ±1 day, month and year and rank the dates where the body fits: the Polish ambulance case was
1979-09-05, not 1980 `[van Utrecht 2023]`; perturb date, time and direction (±1 day, ±1 h, summer
time, mirrored or 90° off) `[Borraz 2023]`; one direction disagreement alone blocked a Venus
identification `[Hendry 1979]`.

## Shape, colour and the words used

### Lights are joined into a body
**Status: consolidated.**
- A string of point sources is conceived as lights or windows on a dark hull ("airship effect"):
  Zond IV (7 of ~30 reported a rocket, cigar or saucer shape, 3 "windows", 17 a "formation"), and the
  1913 procession before the saucer era ("two tiers of lights") `[Hartmann 1968]`.
- Contour and contrast illusions: bright lights darken the field between them, faint stars seem
  eclipsed (Phoenix V, Yukon, Exeter); the Phoenix video shows the lights moving independently
  `[Bullard 2014]`.
- Aircraft reported as saucers, domes, triangles, cigars, a crab, a boomerang `[Hendry 1979]`.
- Zond IV witnesses described the Chiles-Whitted craft of 1948 (cigar, lit windows, flames at the
  rear) `[Hartmann 1968]`: a "windows" or "structure" report must not rule out a fireball.

**Use**: reduce interpretations to primitives before matching ("flying triangle" → "3 moving
lights" `[Passot 2023]`); ask whether any body was actually seen between the lights.

### The stronger the naming word, the more metric detail is volunteered
**Status: consolidated for the detail; the error it implies is argued.**
- On 1,225 gendarmerie testimonies of 18 re-entries, coded by the most specific free term (light <
  phenomenon < shape < object < machine < flying object < UFO), a stronger word goes with more
  metric distances (4 % to 19 %), altitudes (6 % to 42 %), sizes (7 % to 42 %), speeds, "artificial
  details" (0 to about 25 %) and non-banal interpretations, all χ² p < .001; the link holds without the
  1990-11-05 re-entry (38 % of the corpus) and within it alone, and does not depend on age, sex,
  occupation or dependence between witnesses `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 217-241). Those values are wrong
  because the objects were 50-100 km up; how wrong was not measured.
- What was not found, by either study: a link between the word and the distance itself. With
  elevation controlled, word and distance are unrelated (partial tau 0.005) `[Jimenez 1994]` (p. 241),
  as on ~300 other re-entry reports (P = 0.68) `[Rospars 2023]`, whose explanation (one re-entry
  dominating Jimenez's sample) his split analyses do not support. So the word predicts whether a value
  is given, not how far off it is.
- The same progression ("3 points", "3 lights in formation", "3 craft", "cigar with windows")
  `[Hartmann 1968]`; "formation" wrongly implies control `[Bullard 2014]`. An experimenter saying
  "UFO" raising such labels from 18 % to 68 % is attributed to `[GEPAN NT10 1981]` and is not in the
  thesis: to check there.
- A witness's first idea: among 39 witnesses of the 1980-11-11 re-entry questioned months later,
  those who first thought of something strange used stronger words and gave more precise details
  than those who thought of something known and dropped it (χ² p < .02), and specificity grew between
  the gendarmerie report and the survey `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 243-250; small sample, the author's own
  caveat).
- Mechanism: an error appears and persists when a vague or brief stimulus meets a strong but wrong
  schema under a pressing task; trying to identify gives precise and wrong detail, describing gives
  less `[Jimenez 1994]` (ch. 6, after Bruner 1957).
- The longest, most detailed unsolicited Zond IV reports were the most wrong (a cigar with windows);
  correct identifications were brief `[Hartmann 1968]` (qualitative).

**Use**: code the witness's most specific free term on the seven-step scale, from their free account
only, never from answers to questions; from "machine" up, treat volunteered metres, speeds and
artificial details as coming from the schema. Ask what the witness first thought it was, whether they
thought of something known and when they dropped it, and record what they were doing (driving,
trying to identify, describing). Keep the first interpretation verbatim; do not weight detail as
credibility. The implied size (stated distance × angular size) shows what the witness took it for.

### Colour
**Status: contested.**
Main colour consistent for 77 % of re-entry witnesses, trail colour 80 % `[Rospars 2023]`; but
witnesses of the 1962 Green Bank meteor shower gave "almost every colour" `[Drake 1972]` via
`[Smith 1983]`. Colour vision is lost when dark-adapted, so a dim object's colour is inferred
`[Wertheimer 1968]`. The green navigation light is often reported blue `[Hendry 1979]`.

**Use**: down-weight colour for brief, bright or dim nocturnal stimuli; map a reported blue light to
green in the aircraft model.

### Stereotypes and vocabulary
**Status: consolidated (two independent drawing studies), single study for vocabulary.**
- People who claim a sighting draw fewer stereotype features (domes, portholes, legs) than
  non-witnesses `[Haines 1979b]`; independent test by drawing `[GEPAN NT15 1983]`.
- 206 real reports against 475 descriptions of photos: real reports used "cigar", "starlike",
  "portholes", "pattern of lights", "fiery" far more; photo descriptions used "hat", "saucer",
  "oval" `[Shepard 1968]`.

## Memory over time

### Retelling and delay
**Status: consolidated.**
- The ratio of correct to wrong details fell from 3.5 at the first questioning to 1.39 at the second
  and lower at the third; in calm conditions, almost two errors of appearance per scene (188 %)
  `[Sharps 2012]`, `[Sharps 2009]` (mock-crime studies), after `[Bartlett 1932]`.
- Meteor witnesses: about half clearly wrong after 1 day, 3/4 after 2 days, 10 % right after 4 days
  `[Drake 1972]` via `[Smith 1983]` (anecdotal, second-hand).
- Once a conception forms it resists correction `[Hartmann 1968]`.

**Use**: rate a report by the delay between sighting and interview; keep the first interpretation
separately and verbatim; weigh the earliest version most.

### Version drift
**Status: consolidated (many cases).**
Keep every dated version of an account and compare them field by field: sizes growing across
retellings `[Magin 2011]`; "almost disklike" became "silver disc" and the direction reversed between
publications `[Oberg 1994]`; Exeter illustrated as grape-like lights, then a metal saucer with
portholes, then a red disc `[Bullard 2014]`; site, date and number of missiles moving between versions
`[Carlson 2023]`; secondary sources report fewer straight trajectories than the witnesses, who deny
the zigzags the press gave them `[Besse 1980]`.

## Influences on the account

### Interview method
**Status: consolidated.**
Open questions distort less than closed or leading ones; interviewer bias `[Rosenthal 1966]`
`[Wertheimer 1968]`; the cognitive interview recovers 25-35 % more correct content `[Memon 2010]`
via `[Ickinger 2023]`; multiple-choice questions before a free account contaminate it
`[Callahan 2023]`; questionnaire items that do not fit the stimulus ("edges fuzzy or sharp?" for a
point source) produce meaningless answers `[Hartmann 1968]`; investigators ask questions attuned to
their own expectations `[Bullard 2014]`; error enters at the observation, the verbal relay and the
write-up `[Hendry 1979]`.

**Use**: store the questions with the answers, and the witness's words apart from the investigator's
summary; allow "cannot tell".

### Recognition before description
**Status: argued for UFO witnesses, measured for recognition memory.**
Picture recognition over 98 % correct right away and 92 % after a week `[Shepard 1967]`. Proposed
protocol: a free account with no cues, then a choice in a standardised, pre-tested set of images
with decoys, then an optional reconstruction; agreement between witnesses is then testable against
chance `[Shepard 1968]`. Non-verbal answers (pointing, drawing, on-site reconstruction) are measurable
and distorted differently from words `[Rospars 2007]`, after `[GEPAN NT8 1981]`.

**Use**: UFO@home is this reconstruction. Show a fixed, versioned set of rendered views only after
the free account; record pointed azimuth and elevation on site, calibrated on a known reference.

### Media, fiction and expectation
**Status: consolidated.**
- Several Zond IV witnesses found it unusual only after TV coverage `[Hartmann 1968]`; the Carmel
  (NY) wave of 1976 began with Antares in binoculars and spread through press and TV `[Hendry 1979]`;
  the French peak of March 1974 followed a ministerial interview and an order to write reports
  `[Besse 1980]`; solid-object reports rose among later Phoenix accounts `[Bullard 2014]`.
- Against a simple fiction effect: no rise in report rate or content after *Close Encounters* (1977)
  `[Hendry 1979]`; for it, cases shortly after a matching TV film or comic `[Robé 2023]`,
  `[Suenaga 2023]`.
- Being told a place is a "UFO hotspot" turned dust specks into spacecraft `[Sharps 2020]`.
- Measured: monthly gendarmerie reports against UFO articles in three national dailies, 1974-1980,
  Spearman 0.44 for the same month and 0.26-0.27 for articles one and two months earlier; in 1954,
  daily sightings against articles r = 0.76, most sightings of 4 October after the evening papers
  (Gaudeau) `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 275-276).

**Use**: date the witness's reading and media exposure against each version; check what was
broadcast or published in the months before.

### Feedback and confidence
**Status: single study** `[Kelley-Romano 2023]`, on the paradigm of `[Bradfield 2005]`.
After confirming feedback, confidence rises while accuracy does not. Keep the certainty stated before
any feedback and log every feedback with its date.

### Hypnosis
**Status: consolidated.**
13 of 27 highly hypnotisable subjects accepted an implanted memory `[Laurence 1983]`; over 70 % of
children accepted an implanted abduction memory without hypnosis `[Otgaar 2009]`; about two thirds
"remembered" events before age 2 `[Marmelstein 1999]` (all via `[French 2023]`); hypnosis is "not
probative" `[Rhine 1968]`. Whether accounts cluster by hypnotist is contested (`[Matheson 1998]`
against `[Bullard 1989]`).

**Use**: record whether each detail first appeared under hypnosis or guided imagery.

## Several witnesses

### N reports are N confirmations only if independent
**Status: consolidated.**
- A group watching an autokinetic light converges on a shared motion `[Sherif 1935]` via
  `[Rhine 1968]`; a woman talked her husband into seeing Venus's "jerky" descent `[Hendry 1979]`.
- 75 % of Hendry's identified cases had several witnesses and were still misperceived; 97 meteor
  reports came from 74 events `[Hendry 1979]`.
- About a quarter of reports give the right diagnosis, a quarter mislead, the rest are too vague;
  1 to 4 agreeing misleading witnesses make a UFO case `[Hartmann 1968]`.
- Phoenix: the "other" UFOs rest on single witnesses; no one saw the solid craft and the separate
  lights at the same moment, one source seen two ways `[Bullard 2014]`.
- GEPAN rated cases on multiplicity and agreement, independence of observation conditions, and
  beliefs strengthened or not (the PESM scale) `[GEPAN NT10 1981]`. On 431 gendarmerie testimonies,
  shared wording appeared only between relatives: being together, one calling the other or knowing
  each other did not raise it `[Askevis 1981]` via `[Jimenez 1994]` (p. 55).
- Co-occurrence in time should outweigh an exact match of descriptions (rr0.org evaluation page).

**Use**: record per witness whether they are related, were together, talked before reporting, saw each other's
drawings, were interviewed separately, reported before or after media coverage, and who pointed the
phenomenon out to whom. Count a feature by its independent reporters. Keep one stimulus id apart from
the report ids (Venus seen from Kansas and California is two reports, one stimulus `[Hendry 1979]`).

### Expected number of witnesses
**Status: single study, order of magnitude** `[Hartmann 1968]` after `[Olivier 1962]`.
Reports scale with N·t (population within sight × duration): about 6×10⁶ person-seconds per report and
5×10⁹ per photograph for bright fireballs; Zond IV gave 78 reports for 23 million people over 2 min.
Over 10 % of Hynek's audiences had seen a UFO, almost none had reported it `[Smith 1983]`.

**Use**: from the population inside the computed visibility footprint and the stated duration, predict
the expected number of reports; a lone witness to a "large low craft over a town" is then anomalous
for its silence.

## The witness and the viewing conditions

### Profession is not reliability
**Status: contested.**
- Pilots misperceive at high rates: Hynek's figures (88-89 % explained for pilots) are quoted
  differently by `[Oberg 1994]` and `[Printy 2023]` and must be checked in `[Hynek 1977]`; a
  world-renowned astronomer and two colleagues took three jets at 20,000 ft for a slow orange light
  `[Hartmann 1968]`; pilots are trained to read any light as an aircraft on collision course
  `[Oberg 1985]`; about 90 % of sincere, sometimes very competent witnesses report identifiable
  objects `[Hendry 1979]`.
- Against: `[Haines 2023]` defends pilot credibility; Blue Book's own figures gave "excellent" reports
  33 % unknowns against 17 % for "poor" ones `[BBSR14 1955]` via `[Hendry 1979]`.
- Expertise matters when it fits the stimulus: one amateur astronomer with a telescope saw aircraft at
  Phoenix `[Bullard 2014]`.
- Sex, age and occupation relate neither to the naming word nor to precision on 1,225 testimonies
  `[Jimenez 1994]` (p. 228), nor do they separate identified from unidentified reports `[Besse 1982]`.

**Use**: record flying experience, stress, visibility, expectation and whether confirmation was
sought, rather than a bonus for occupation.

### Observer state and viewing medium
**Status: consolidated (laboratory findings); case-based for media.**
Dark adaptation, fatigue, alcohol, medication, anxiety, myopia, phosphenes and corneal motes
`[Wertheimer 1968]`, `[Rhine 1968]`; isolation and monotony (long night drives, radar watch, solo
flight) produce lapses and hallucinations `[McFarland 1957]`, `[Mackworth 1950]`, `[Clark 1957]`;
glass, heat haze and jet exhaust distort (an airliner seen through exhaust became a grey disc)
`[Hartmann 1968]`; binoculars on a twinkling star magnify the distortion `[Hendry 1979]`; brightness is
judged against dark adaptation `[Oberg 1994]`; a medical and eye protocol for witnesses `[Walker 1968]`.

**Use**: fields for corrected vision, colour blindness, recent bright exposure, time outdoors,
alcohol or medication, tiredness, optical aid, window or windscreen; add reflection and distortion
candidates when viewed through glass.

### Sleep and private experiences
**Status: consolidated for sleep paralysis; argued for the checklists.**
Sleep paralysis with hypnagogic hallucinations has a 40-50 % lifetime prevalence `[Cheyne 1999]` via
`[Mavrakis 2023]`; 114 of 152 practitioners produced an alien encounter on purpose in lucid dreams
`[Raduga 2021]`, whose eight markers (paralysis, "vibrations", floating, passing through walls,
jumps between places, a forgotten ending...) are untested. Sort "private" experiences (bedroom, alone,
near sleep) from "public" sightings checkable against sky and weather `[Scribner 2023]`,
`[Noll 2023]`; exclude entity-only and voice-only close encounters `[Hendry 1979]`.

## Hearing

### Apparent silence is usually masking, distance or delay
**Status: argued** (the acoustics is textbook; its application to accounts rests on cases).
A familiar ambient noise masks any sound some 10 dB below it (a car with the fan and the radio on is
about 75 dB inside; closed windows remove 15 to over 30 dB); the level drops about 6 dB per doubling
of distance in free field, 1 to 10 dB in practice; the sound of an aircraft at 5,000 m arrives 15-22 s
after its image, by which time it has flown more than 6 km; the high-pitched sound sent ahead of an
approaching aircraft (Doppler) is absorbed fastest (50 dB/km at 4 kHz, 20 °C, 30 % RH); a supersonic
aircraft is audible only inside its Mach cone; wind, fog, snow, terrain shadows and reflections change
level, pitch and apparent direction `[Fedoce 2014]`. A worked case "inspired by a GEIPAN
investigation": a driver passing under a wide-body aircraft 250 m up, 8 km after take-off, predicted
78 dB outside against the ~90 dB needed to be heard in the car `[Fedoce 2014]`. Consistent with 91 %
of low advertising planes not heard `[Hendry 1979]`.

**Use**: an interpreted body (aircraft, helicopter, balloon) carries a reference source level and
spectrum; propagate it to the observer (spherical spreading, ISO 9613-1 absorption from the ERA5
temperature and humidity, delay at the local speed of sound, Doppler, Mach cone, terrain line of sight,
wind and inversion from the archived upper-air profiles); compare the predicted level with a declared
listening situation (outdoors, in a car with windows closed, radio on...) and the ~10 dB masking rule;
an `Assessor` reports whether the stated silence, or the stated sound, fits the explanation. The
account's `SightingSound.volume` stays relative: the decibels are the interpretation's prediction,
never the witness's statement. Later: a binaural (HRTF) playback of the predicted sound from the
observer's place; locating a source from arrival-time differences between several witnesses'
recordings.

### Sound localisation is coarse, and distance is underestimated
**Status: argued** (figures from a synthesis; to be checked in the primary psychoacoustic literature).
Azimuth uncertainty a few degrees ahead, about 10° on the sides, 5° behind; elevation about 10° ahead,
15-20° overhead, 20-30° behind; distance underestimated by a factor of 2-3; sounds of interest are
placed in front and ambient noise behind; a high-pitched source seems higher than a low one at the same
height (around 8 kHz from above; 700 Hz-2 kHz and 10-15 kHz from behind); sounds below 2 kHz seem more
distant `[Fedoce 2014]`, after `[Gabriel]`. The primary source to read is `[Blauert 1997]`.

**Use**: give a stated sound direction and distance an uncertainty cone, wider in elevation and behind
the head; do not treat a mismatch between heard and seen direction as a contradiction when the sound
delay explains it.

### The observer's hearing
**Status: argued.** Presbycusis shows from 18 in high frequencies and takes the sibilants first; the
cocktail-party effect weakens with age; sensitivity peaks around 4 kHz; hyperacusis about 2 % of the
population; perceived loudness is lower for an expected loud sound `[Fedoce 2014]`. Typical threshold
shift by age and frequency: `[ISO 7029]`; equal-loudness contours: `[ISO 226]`.

**Use**: fields for age, known hearing problems, musical practice and usual noise surroundings; for the
sound heard, apparent direction, loudness, pitch, duration and what it sounded like (a `SaidText`
likeness: "like a transformer"), the list recommended to GEIPAN investigators `[Fedoce 2014]`; filter
the predicted sound by the typical age-related loss before the audibility check.

## Candidate checks from date and place

### Base rates
**Status: single study per sample; the rates depend on the sample.**
Of 1,024 identified night lights `[Hendry 1979]`: stars and planets 35 %, **advertising planes** 22 %,
aircraft 19 % (a quarter of them helicopters), meteors and re-entries 11 %, satellites, the Moon (2 %),
prank balloons, searchlights, balloons, launches. The Moon was 21 % of 138 French cases of 1976
`[Maillot 2019]` (the Saros team; `[Magin 2023]`, `[Maillot 2023]` and `[Robé 2023]` rest on it).

**Use**: prior weights for ranking candidates, per region and period.

### Signatures
- **Aircraft and advertising planes** (consolidated): no sound for 91 % of low advertising planes,
  so silence never rules out an aircraft; slow circling over a town, apparent rotation, hovering,
  "sudden departure" when the sign switches off `[Hendry 1979]`; formation flights `[Bullard 2014]`;
  airfields and extended runway centrelines `[Delaval 2023]`, `[Hendry 1979]`. A witness's "it could
  not be an aircraft" does not rule one out: of 62 aircraft and helicopter testimonies, those using
  strong words gave distances, sizes and details that were mostly wrong, and 263 unidentified
  gendarmerie reports sit next to aircraft and helicopters in a factor analysis (under 1 km, 2-10 m,
  near the horizon, medium duration) `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 282-285).
- **Meteors and re-entries** (consolidated): meteors ~15 km/s, seconds; debris half as fast and longer
  `[Young 2023]`; re-entries slower, flat, usually heading east, over 10 s `[Hendry 1979]`; many lights
  in formation, near-horizontal, silent, witnesses spread over hundreds of km `[Oberg 2023]`,
  `[Hartmann 1968]`; decay catalogues within ±2 h; fireball networks within ±1 day and ±30 min.
- **Launches, plumes, fuel dumps** (case-based): Plesetsk and Baikonur launches, dumps about an hour
  after launch, a plume sunlit while the observer is in twilight `[Oberg 1985]`, `[Oberg 1994]`,
  `[Campo Pérez 2023]`; reported manoeuvres may be the plume's wind-sheared history `[Oberg 1994]`.
- **Lanterns and balloons** (case-based): home-made prank balloons, warm months, 18:00-02:30,
  1-15 min, orange, dripping or falling embers at the end, drifting with the wind aloft
  `[Hendry 1979]`; an under-inflated Mylar balloon warming up `[Maillot 2023]`.
- **Flares** (case-based): lights appearing and going out one by one, low over a range
  `[Callahan 2023]`; a deliberate flare test over Clearwater (1965-07-04) is a calibration set whose
  results are not on rr0.org.
- **Lightning and ball lightning** (single study): lightning-location data within ~5 km and ±10 min;
  strong positive cloud-to-ground strokes correlate with ball lightning `[Keul 2018]`.
- **Small near objects** (case-based): a kite 27 m up seen as a giant triangle `[Martins 2023]`.
- **Radar** (case-based): all five of Hendry's radar-visual cases were false matches; check with the
  operator directly `[Hendry 1979]`; "no radar contact" can mean distance, not speed `[Oberg 1994]`.

### Kinematic feasibility of an explanation
**Status: argued** `[Shough 2012]` via `[Bullard 2014]`.
For Exeter's lights to be seen separately the tanker proposed had to be within about a mile, and to
stay in view as long as reported it would have flown about 10 mph. Test every candidate for angular
resolution and time on screen: this rejects bad explanations as well as bad testimony. An explanation
must not deny well-established facts (a flight log, a fixed geometry) `[Rospars 2007]`; different
stimuli give the same report, so rank candidates and keep "unsolved" apart from "anomalous"
`[Oberg 1985]`.

## Scoring and indexes

- **Data sufficiency** (consolidated): 32 % of one pilot catalogue has no date or time `[Printy 2023]`;
  brightness unspecified in 65-68 % of Blue Book cases, shape in about a quarter `[BBSR14 1955]` via
  `[Hendry 1979]`; 39 % of Gendarmerie reports omit the weather `[Besse 1980]`. Score completeness
  (date, time, direction, elevation, angular size, duration, manner of disappearance) and flag a case
  below the minimum.
- **Selection biases the variables** `[Besse 1980]`: selecting on credibility biases profession,
  witness count and age; on strangeness, shape, trajectory and speed. The reporting channel censors
  the sample (lone witnesses and women under-represented in Gendarmerie reports). Store expert review
  as a parameter, not a filter; record the channel.
- **Explained cases as the control group** (consolidated): identified reports come from the same
  people for the same reasons `[Hendry 1979]`, and do not differ from unidentified ones in witnesses,
  conditions, or most described features (shape, size, brightness, colour, speed, noise)
  `[Besse 1982]` via `[Jimenez 1994]` (p. 50); a feature is anomalous only if it differs from the same
  feature in explained cases `[Leduc 2023]`; time of day is not evidence (22:00 peak in every file)
  `[Besse 1980]`, `[Leduc 2023]`.
- **Residue as a function of time** (argued): old cases get explained while new ones fill the pool
  `[Ares de Blas 2023]`; the best cases are "long ago and far away" `[Oberg 1985]`.
- **Indexes**: Hynek's strangeness and probability `[Hynek 1972]`; Vallee's SVP; the subjectivity
  index `[Ballester-Olmos 1984]` (weights are the authors' judgement, never calibrated); GEPAN's PESM
  scale `[GEPAN NT10 1981]`; a graded case status (explained as known, possible, probable, difficult)
  `[Rospars 2007]`; Hendry's four grades of unexplained cases `[Hendry 1979]`; GEPAN's A (identified),
  B (probably identified), C (too little detail or coherence), D (unidentified despite detail).
- **Reporting fraction** (measured order of magnitude): about 10 % of sightings reached the French
  authorities, and 87 % of observers told only family and friends `[Lee 1969]`, `[Besse 1980]` via
  `[Jimenez 1994]` (pp. 18-22).
- **Deception cues** work only on samples: 3 of 18 verbal cues discriminate (detail, coherence,
  admitting memory gaps), 78 % accuracy `[Porter 1996]`; about 70 % at best `[Matsumoto 2015]` via
  `[Martins 2023]`; mistaken witnesses pass any lie test `[Hendry 1979]`. Stated doubt and spontaneous
  corrections are positive signs.
- **Records against claims** (case-based): contemporaneous medical and physical records, null tests
  logged `[Nickell 2023]`, `[Posner 2023]`; the physical-evidence chain (8 steps, each about 0.5) makes a
  usable trace case rare `[Rospars 2007]`.
